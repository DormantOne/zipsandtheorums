Ternary champions

This tab uses the learning routine from Walker Precision Champions. Each brain is 16, 32 or 64 identical ternary protoneurons with no layers: every unit has three input slots weighted −1, 0 or +1, reads sensors or any unit (itself included), and outputs −1, 0 or +1. Six units vote on how strongly to promote or demote each candidate move. The bot’s tactics stay fixed. Nothing is differentiated, so there are no gradients anywhere.

Six independent founders start the run. Each generation, 10 candidates play the same games. Four of them are mirrored precision probes: pairs of brains that nudge hidden float shadows of the ternary genes in opposite directions, so the better one teaches its parent. The rest are mutants of the current brain or of a champion, some crossed with a second champion. A candidate replaces the current brain only if it beats it on those games. Every other generation, brains face a fixed validation suite and an archive keeps the best brain for each style of play.

The rules

The board has 11 cells along each of four axes, 14,641 cells in all. The first stone goes in the very centre. After that, every stone must share a face with a stone already on the board: it has to sit one step along x, y, z or w from an existing stone. Each cell has eight such neighbours. There is no gravity; the cluster grows wherever the players push it.

You win with a straight line of your own stones, eight long by default. Lines can run along any of 40 directions: the four axes, plus every diagonal you get by stepping along two, three or all four axes at once. On this board that makes 57,840 possible winning lines for eight in a row.

Doors

Diagonal stones don’t touch each other, so a diagonal line can be one stone from finished while its last cell is still unplayable, because nothing touches it yet. Whoever places a stone next to that cell opens the door, and the line’s owner wins on their next move. Both players learn to tiptoe around these cells, and the board marks them with a dashed ring.

How the bot thinks

Every bot shares the same exact tactics. It takes a winning move if it has one, otherwise blocks one, never opens a door, plays a move that creates two threats at once, and blocks the opponent’s chance to do the same. Everything else comes from 14 weights. Every possible line through a cell is scored by how many stones it still needs and whose stones are in it, and the bot plays the cell whose lines add up to the most. Those running totals are updated incrementally, so a move takes a fraction of a millisecond even with tens of thousands of lines in play.

The weights are keyed by how many stones a line still needs, so “two to go” means the same thing at every length. That made it possible to train the seven-in-a-row bot starting from the six-in-a-row champion. The ternary champion adds a small brain on top of all this, described at the end of this page.

What evolution found

Evolved from scratch, the bots for five and six in a row beat the hand-tuned bot in almost every game. At seven, the strongest bot came from tuning the hand-tuned bot’s own 14 weights in games against it, and it won 36 of 40 against the original. At eight, evolution splits. Trained from scratch, it becomes a pure defender that draws nearly everything and never wins. Trained from the seven champion, it becomes an attacker that beats untrained play every time but is out-defended by the hand-tuned bot. The attacker is the one you play against, because the defender would only stall. When almost every game is a draw, there is very little signal to learn from, and that, more than anything else, is what makes eight in a row in 4D a strange game.

Does a bigger brain help?

I tried giving the scorer a hidden layer: four tanh neurons that read how many nearly finished lines of each kind pass through a cell, so it could learn combinations such as two lines that are both two stones from done. It started as an exact copy of the hand-tuned bot, and evolution was allowed to change only the new neurons. A control run tuned the plain 14 weights with the same number of games.

In a rowWith hidden layerPlain weights
7won 28, lost 12won 36, lost 4
8won 5, drew 31, lost 4won 3, drew 34, lost 3

Each line is 40 fresh games against the hand-tuned bot. The extra neurons did not beat simple tuning at seven, and at eight neither version could break a careful defender. The bottleneck is not how the bot scores a move but how far it looks ahead: every bot here plays one move deep, and beating a good blocker at eight would take searching through sequences of forcing moves.

Ternary champions

The Evolve tab uses the learning routine from Walker Precision Champions. A brain is 16 identical ternary protoneurons with no layers and no gradients, evolved with mirrored precision probes, mutation, crossover, a champion pool, immigrants and restarts from champions when progress stalls. A champion does not replace the tactics or the line scores. For each candidate move, its six voting units add up to a number between −6 and +6, which multiplies that move’s line score by anything from a third to three times. A brain whose units all stay at 0 plays exactly like the bot underneath it.

Results on fresh games against the hand-tuned bot, after a few minutes of evolution at each length:

Eight in a row is 40 games; the others are 20. At eight, the bot underneath is the hand-tuned bot itself, and the ternary champion is the only bot here that beats it at all. It makes about 18 forcing threats a game to the hand-tuned bot’s 11 and wins 16 of 40 games that would otherwise have been draws. It also overreaches and loses 9, so its edge is real but modest. At shorter lengths, the evolved weights underneath already win almost every game, so the ternary layer has little to add and overfits its small validation suite. That is why the opponent menu offers the ternary champion only at eight. Precision probe pairs produced the largest share of accepted improvements at every length.

Two other things changed along with the brain. Draws are now graded by which side made more forcing threats instead of all counting as one half, and the archive keeps several styles of play alive. I haven’t yet separated how much each of the three contributes.
