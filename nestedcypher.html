Nest Cipher 6: jump + lattice, the hybrid handshake

Two strangers agree on a secret in public two ways at once. The jump handshake hops around a prime-cornered polygon of the nest. The lattice handshake hides small noise inside a 640-dimensional grid seeded by the nest's own area digits, with no prime anywhere. Both secrets are mixed into one. Then every letter rides its own secret nest polygon. A quantum computer could someday break the jump half, but not, as far as anyone knows, the lattice half.

1. The jump handshake, small enough to watch

The 41-gon is depth 37 of the nest. 41 is prime, so vertices 1–40 form a closed loop under multiplication. One jump means: from vertex v, go to vertex v × g, wrapping around 41. With g = 6 you visit all 40 vertices before coming back: 1 → 6 → 36 → 11 → 25 → 27 → 39 → … with no pattern to follow back.

StepAliceBobEve sees

  1. Round 1: Alice starts on vertex 1 and makes a secret jumps of size g. She announces where she lands, A. Bob does the same with b jumps and announces B.
  2. Round 2: the other person's landing vertex becomes the new jump size. Alice makes her same a jumps, each ×B. Bob makes his b jumps, each ×A. Both land on the same vertex S.
  3. Eve sees A and B. To get S she needs a jump count back from a landing vertex: the discrete logarithm problem. Ordinary computers can't do that on a polygon with a 617-digit number of corners. A large quantum computer could, using Shor's algorithm. That's why the lattice half exists.

2. The lattice handshake: noise on a grid, read off the square's corners

The hard problem. Take a grid (a lattice) and a point that sits near one of its grid points, nudged by a little noise. Which grid point is it near? In 2 dimensions, as in the picture, you can see the answer at a glance. In 640 dimensions, nobody knows a fast way, with an ordinary computer or a quantum one. This is the Learning With Errors problem (LWE).

No prime anywhere. Every number lives on a dial of 215 = 32,768 positions. That's a power of two, not a prime, and it doesn't need to be one: the hardness comes from the noise and the 640 dimensions, not from the size of the dial.

Reading the key off the square. Bob places each piece of the key on one of the four corners of a square, the nest's very first polygon (depth 0), drawn on the 32,768-position dial. The noise nudges each point a little off its corner. Alice rounds to the nearest corner. As long as the noise stays under 45°, she reads every piece exactly.

The steps (FrodoKEM-640 sizes)

  1. The public grid A: 640 × 640 numbers on the dial. It's generated from the nest's digits plus a fresh random seed, so nobody can have hand-picked it.
  2. Alice: picks a secret S made of small numbers (mostly −3 to 3) and small noise E. She publishes B = A·S + E. Without the noise, simple algebra would recover S. With the noise, it's the lattice problem.
  3. Bob: picks his own small secret S′ and noise. He publishes B′ = S′·A + noise, and C = S′·B + noise + the key placed on the square's corners.
  4. Alice: computes C − B′·S. Almost everything cancels, because S′·A·S appears on both sides. What's left is the key plus a little noise, and rounding to the nearest corner removes the noise.
  5. Eve sees A, B, B′, and C. To read the key she'd need S or S′, which means solving the lattice problem.

The nest's digits

The share of the square still inside the nest's 60-gon (depth 56), computed exactly from the closed-form step ratios on your original page to 1,024 decimal places. It plays the role π plays in the standard jump prime: "nothing up my sleeve" digits that anyone can recompute. It is not what makes the lattice hard. Any honestly chosen digits would do, and the fresh seed is mixed in too.

This session's 64 dials: the real lattice handshake

Each dial is one of the 64 numbers Alice gets from C − B′·S. The four corners of the square are the four possible values of each 2-bit piece of the key. The dot is where Alice's number landed, and the ring marks the corner Bob placed it on. 64 dials × 2 bits = a 128-bit lattice key.

3. This session's hybrid handshake

Alice sends her jump vertex plus her lattice key (seed + B). Bob replies with his jump vertex plus B′ and C. Each side combines the jump secret and the lattice secret, along with a fingerprint of everything that was sent, into one shared key (HKDF-SHA-256). To read messages, Eve would have to break both.

The walk for this letter

Landing vertices on the wire

  1. Pick the polygon. The secret stream chooses a nest polygon for this letter, from the 40-gon to the 60-gon. Vertices 0–39 hold A–Z, 0–9, space, period, comma, question mark. Any vertices beyond 39 are empty seats.
  2. Walk. Start on the letter's vertex p, walk forward s secret segments, then the chain. The landing vertex is L = (p + s + chain) mod m.
  3. Chain. The next letter starts from L + p, so every letter depends on every letter before it.
  4. Seal. HMAC-SHA-256 covers the nonce and every landing vertex. Change one, and Bob refuses to decrypt anything.

Is it quantum safe?

Believed to be, and nobody can be certain. No one has proven that any public-key method is safe against quantum computers. "Quantum safe" means that decades of work by many researchers have found no fast quantum attack on these problems.

PieceAgainst a large quantum computer
Jump handshake (discrete logarithm)Broken by Shor's algorithm, once a large enough machine exists.
Lattice handshake (LWE)No known quantum attack. A 2024 paper claimed one, and other researchers found a bug in it within about a week. The claim was withdrawn.
Hybrid of the twoSafe as long as either half holds. This is how browsers and national guidance deploy post-quantum keys today.
AES-256 (polygon and step choices), HMAC-SHA-256 (seal)Quantum search roughly halves their strength, which still leaves about 128 bits. Considered safe.
The nest geometry, walks, and chainForm, not strength. They neither help nor hurt.

Limits of this page specifically

  • Teaching code, not the standard. It uses FrodoKEM-640's sizes and error table, but it isn't byte-compatible with FrodoKEM, and it lacks FrodoKEM's extra protection for reused keys. That's acceptable only because every handshake here uses brand-new keys. It hasn't been audited and isn't hardened against timing attacks.
  • Level 1. The 640 size targets NIST level 1, roughly AES-128 strength. Germany's BSI recommends the larger FrodoKEM-976 or 1344 for long-term secrets.
  • No identity check except the safety number. Compare it out loud.
  • Alice and Bob share one page for the demo, and keys vanish on reload. Not for PHI.

What's yours, and what's standard

  • Yours: the nest as the frame, letters as polygon vertices, segment walks, the chain, a secret polygon per letter, the jump picture of the handshake, the nest's area digits as the grid's source, and reading the key off the square's corners.
  • Standard underneath: Diffie–Hellman (1976) over the RFC 3526 prime; Learning With Errors (Regev, 2005) with FrodoKEM-640's sizes; hybrid key mixing; HKDF, AES-256-CTR, and HMAC-SHA-256 from the browser's Web Crypto.
