From the square: circle, corner triangles, and the rest

A pentagon goes inside a square, a hexagon inside the pentagon, and so on, each as large as possible while touching only with vertices. Every gap left behind is either a full corner triangle, where one side of the inner polygon bridges a corner and touches at both ends, or a larger irregular piece. What stays in the middle shrinks to a circle.

The exact steps

Every best fit touches with four vertices in two neighbouring pairs and is mirror-symmetric, which pins each step to a formula. With m the outer polygon's sides, the new circumradius divided by the old is

m even: cos(π/m) ÷ [cos(π/(m(m+1))) · cos(π/(2(m+1)))]
m odd: sin(2π/m) · cos(π/(2m)) ÷ [sin(π(m+2)/(m(m+1))) + cos(π/m) · sin(π/(m+1))]

The circle's share of the square is π/2 times the product of the squares of these ratios. That equals 2πK²G², where K = 0.1149420448… is the Kepler–Bouwkamp constant and G = 1.3505045336… is the extra gain from letting each polygon shift off-center.
